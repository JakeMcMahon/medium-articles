How PostHog Competes With Amplitude and Mixpanel (And Why Open-Source Matters for Analytics)

PostHog has been open-source since 2020. As of 2026-01-26, the PostHog/posthog repo has 30,983 GitHub stars and 2,218 forks (GitHub).

This isn't a "feature checklist" story. The biggest differences between PostHog, Amplitude, and Mixpanel show up in four places: what you pay for, how you query, what you can connect, and how you prove compliance.

TL;DR

If you skim one section, skim this:

  • Open-source changes risk: You can inspect code and deployment options instead of trusting a black box (GitHub).
  • Pricing is structurally different: PostHog and Mixpanel are event-based; Amplitude is MTU-based (PostHog pricing, Mixpanel pricing, Amplitude pricing).
  • PostHog is built for joining product + business data: SQL access plus a built-in data warehouse means fewer export pipelines (SQL, Data warehouse).
  • HIPAA reality: If you need a BAA, PostHog documents when they provide one for PostHog Cloud (HIPAA compliance, BAA generator).

Why Open-Source Matters for Analytics

Closed-source analytics asks you to trust what you can't see. You ship events to a vendor, and somewhere in the middle your data is transformed, sampled, filtered, and stored behind a black box.

Open-source flips the relationship: instead of "trust us," it's "verify it." You can inspect how events are processed, validate what's collected (and what isn't), and prove your setup meets internal standards or external requirements.

This matters most when you're accountable for privacy and security (healthcare, fintech, government), but it also matters any time you want to avoid lock-in or build analytics that joins product behavior to business reality.

What Actually Differs Between PostHog, Amplitude, and Mixpanel

Here's the practical comparison that tends to matter once you're past the "basic charts" stage.

  • Pricing unit: PostHog and Mixpanel are event-based; Amplitude is MTU-based (PostHog pricing, Mixpanel pricing, Amplitude pricing).
  • SQL-style querying in-product: PostHog supports HogQL/SQL; Mixpanel offers JQL (in "maintenance mode"); Amplitude's model is often warehouse/integration-driven (PostHog SQL, Mixpanel JQL).
  • Feature flags + experiments: PostHog includes them; Mixpanel says feature flagging + experimentation is Enterprise; Amplitude positions feature flags as included (PostHog feature flags, PostHog experiments, Mixpanel experimentation, Amplitude pricing).
  • Session replay: PostHog includes 5k recordings free; Mixpanel includes 10K replays on Free and 20K on Growth; Amplitude lists 1,000/month on Starter and 10,000/month on Plus (PostHog pricing, Mixpanel pricing, Amplitude pricing).
  • Data retention: PostHog states 1 year on free, 7 years on paid; Mixpanel documents a 2-year default retention update effective 2025-09-01 (PostHog pricing, Mixpanel retention update).

Points worth borrowing from PostHog's own head-to-head comparisons (useful even if you don't choose PostHog):

  • All-in-one platform: PostHog positions itself as an "all-in-one platform" (product analytics, web analytics, session replay, feature flags, experiments, surveys, error tracking, and a built-in data warehouse) (PostHog vs Mixpanel, PostHog vs Amplitude).
  • Who it's built for: PostHog frames the key buyer split as engineering/product vs marketing/growth: they emphasize developer support, documented APIs, SQL query builder, and open-source transparency (PostHog vs Mixpanel, PostHog vs Amplitude).
  • Pricing models: PostHog explains pricing models in plain terms: usage-based vs MTU-based for Amplitude, and (even when both are event-priced) how user identification can change cost dynamics (PostHog vs Amplitude, PostHog vs Mixpanel).
  • Warehouse architecture: PostHog calls out a real architectural difference: Amplitude can run directly on top of your data warehouse, while PostHog syncs to/from warehouses rather than sitting on top of them (PostHog vs Amplitude).
  • Data quality: For data quality, PostHog recommends deploying a reverse proxy to reduce tracking blockers and improve capture, and offers a managed reverse proxy option (PostHog vs Mixpanel).

1) What you pay for (events vs MTUs vs add-ons)

Pricing isn't just cost. It determines what you can afford to measure as your team scales.

  • PostHog: Usage-based pricing for Product Analytics starts from $0.00005/event and includes a free allowance of the first 1 million events. The pricing page also shows free allowances for things like session replay and feature flag requests (PostHog pricing).
  • Mixpanel: Growth includes 1M monthly events free and then $0.28 per 1K events after, and includes plan-specific limits like 20K monthly session replays free. Mixpanel's free plan lists up to 5 saved reports and 10K monthly session replays (Mixpanel pricing).
  • Amplitude: Pricing is structured around MTUs (monthly tracked users), with Plus starting at $49/mo. Amplitude's free tier highlights 50K MTUs (and up to 10M events) (Amplitude pricing).

Practical implication: if your product generates lots of events per user (or you track aggressively), event-based pricing can ramp quickly. If you have lots of low-activity users, MTU-based pricing can ramp quickly.

One nuance teams miss: MTU pricing can feel cheap until you instrument "helpful" tracking for lots of lightly-active users. Event pricing can feel cheap until you add autocapture + high-frequency events. The right answer is the one that matches your actual product behavior.

If you're choosing between event-based platforms, it can still matter how pricing treats anonymous usage. In its Mixpanel comparison, PostHog notes that both are event-priced, but Mixpanel charges the same for anonymous and identified events while PostHog differentiates them (PostHog vs Mixpanel).

2) Querying and joining product + business data

PostHog is unusually direct about SQL-first workflows:

  • SQL access: PostHog supports querying with SQL/HogQL (SQL).
  • Data warehouse: You can sync data sources into PostHog's data warehouse and query across them (Data warehouse).

Mixpanel supports custom querying via JQL, which Mixpanel notes is "currently in maintenance mode" (Mixpanel JQL).

The difference shows up when you need analysis like "product behavior + revenue + account attributes" and you don't want to maintain exports just to answer basic questions.

Concrete example: PostHog's HogQL is designed for writing queries that join your events with warehouse tables you sync in (for example, Stripe customers) (HogQL, Data warehouse query).

SELECT
  events.distinct_id,
  count() AS event_count
FROM events
INNER JOIN prod_stripe_customer
  ON events.distinct_id = prod_stripe_customer.email
GROUP BY events.distinct_id
ORDER BY event_count DESC

If your operating model is "everything lives in the warehouse," PostHog notes Amplitude can run directly on top of your data warehouse, while PostHog syncs to/from warehouses rather than sitting on top of them (PostHog vs Amplitude).

3) Experimentation and feature flags

If analytics is part of how you ship product changes, flags + experiments determine whether you have one loop (build, ship, measure) or three tools glued together.

  • PostHog: Built-in feature flags and experiments (Feature flags, Experiments).
  • Mixpanel: Mixpanel states "Experimentation 2.0 and Feature Flagging are available for Enterprise plans" (Mixpanel experimentation).
  • Amplitude: Pricing pages position feature flags as included (Amplitude pricing).

If you're using analytics to ship and measure product changes, this is the difference between "analytics plus a separate rollout system" and a tighter loop.

4) Cohorts and governance limits (the stuff that bites later)

If you're comparing plans, watch for "soft" limits like chart saves and cohort limits. They're easy to miss, and they change what teams can do day-to-day.

One example that's explicit in Amplitude's own announcement: the Plus plan supports "Create up to 5 cohorts" for cohort analysis (Amplitude Plus announcement).

5) Data retention is not the same across vendors

Retention is easy to ignore until you need long-range baselines (or a compliance conversation). Then it suddenly matters.

  • PostHog: "Data ... is guaranteed to be retained for 7 years on any paid plan and 1 year on a free plan" (PostHog pricing).
  • Mixpanel: Mixpanel documents a shift to a 2-year default data retention period, effective September 1, 2025 (Mixpanel changelog).

Retention rules change. Treat this as a procurement checklist item, not an assumption.

The Compliance Constraint (Healthcare-Friendly Analytics Without Guesswork)

HIPAA doesn't mean "no analytics." It means you must be deliberate about what data you capture, how you process it, and what agreements you have in place.

PostHog documents their HIPAA stance and BAA availability:

  • PostHog offers BAAs for PostHog Cloud for users with Boost, Scale or Enterprise add-ons (PostHog and HIPAA compliance).
  • They provide a BAA generator to create a Business Associate Agreement for countersignature (BAA generator).

In PostHog's own comparisons, they also describe BAAs as part of their platform packages/add-ons, alongside items like SSO enforcement and a managed reverse proxy (PostHog vs Mixpanel, PostHog vs Amplitude).

The tactical takeaway: pick your analytics stack based on what you can prove and control (data minimization, redaction, and clear vendor terms), not what a marketing page implies.

A Practical Migration Pattern (If You're Switching Tools)

Whether you're migrating from Mixpanel, Amplitude, or something homegrown, the cleanest approach is usually:

  1. Dual-write: Send events to both systems temporarily.
  2. Rebuild the few dashboards that matter: Keep parity tight (activation funnel, retention, key feature adoption).
  3. Validate definitions: Event names, user identity, and any "group/account" logic.
  4. Cut over: Move decision-making to the new dashboards; retire the old tracking gradually.

The goal is not "copy every chart." It's "recreate the decisions your team actually makes."

When PostHog Is (and Isn't) the Right Choice

PostHog tends to win when you need: - Developer-friendly workflows (SQL, data warehouse joins, instrument-and-iterate). - Transparent security/compliance posture (especially in regulated contexts). - One stack for analytics plus replay plus flags/experiments.

You should still consider alternatives when: - Your organization is strongly standardized on a different analytics ecosystem. - You want a very specific enterprise bundle (advanced governance, predictive audiences, etc.) and you're willing to pay for it.
